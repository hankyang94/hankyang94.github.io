PSD Cone Projection: A Spectral Operator

Bottleneck for scaling first-order methods for SDP: PSD cone projection

Recall the definition of PSD cone projection:

\[\PSDproj(X):=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{Y\in\Sn_+^n}\frac12\norm{Y-X}_F^2.\]

Closed-form solution : let \(X=Q\Lambda Q^\top\) be the spectral decomposition.

\[\PSDproj(X)=Q\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\max\{\lambda_1,0\},\ldots,\max\{\lambda_n,0\}\bigr)Q^\top.\]

Idea: approximate \(\frelu\) with another function whose spectral operator is easier to evaluate?

Polynomial Filtering

Suppose \(p(x)\) is a univariate polynomial filter such that \(p(x)\approx\frelu(x)\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}\PSDproj(X)&=Q\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\frelu(\lambda_1),\ldots,\frelu(\lambda_n)\bigr)Q^\top\\&\approx Q\operatorname{diag}\bigl(p(\lambda_1),\ldots,p(\lambda_n)\bigr)Q^\top\\&=\blue{p(X)}.\end{aligned}\]

Evaluating \(p(X)\) only requires general matrix-matrix multiplication (GEMM)!

However, \(\frelu\) is nonsmooth at \(x=0\). An accurate approximation (e.g., using Chebyshev polynomials) needs a high-degree \(p(x)\) and many GEMMs.

Need a high-degree polynomial \(p(x)\) that can be evaluated with a small number of GEMMs!

Composite Polynomial Filtering

Restrict \(p(x)\) to be a composition of low-degree polynomials:

\[p(x)=f_T\circ f_{T-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_1(x),\qquad\deg(f_i)\leq d_i,\quad i=1,\ldots,T.\]

Degree at most \(\prod_{i=1}^T d_i\); evaluating \(p(X)\) takes at most \(\sum_{i=1}^T d_i\) GEMMs.

Finding the “best” composite polynomial filter (fix \((d_1,\ldots,d_T)\)):

\[\inf_{f_1,\ldots,f_T}\ \max_{x\in[-1,1]}\left|f_T\circ\cdots\circ f_1(x)-\frelu(x)\right|\quad\text{s.t. }f_i\in\mathbb R_{d_i}[x],\ i=1,\ldots,T.\tag{1}\]

To the best of our knowledge, (1) cannot be efficiently solved.

A Two-stage Strategy

  • Optimize a surrogate objective of (1) using dynamic programming .
  • Gradient-based refinement using the true objective (1).

Benchmark and Evaluation: Setup

Hardware. NVIDIA B200 GPUs (\(192\) GB RAM) running CUDA 12.9 and NVIDIA H100 GPUs (\(80\) GB RAM) running CUDA 12.4. On B200, enable the BF16x9 emulation introduced in CUDA 12.9 for the Blackwell architecture.

Methods. Compare GPU implementations. For a \(10{,}000\times10{,}000\) dense symmetric matrix, MATLAB eig takes about 15 seconds on a high-performance workstation; NVIDIA’s cuSOLVER takes about 1 second on B200 and H100.

  • cuSOLVER FP64: classical factorization with cuSOLVER (ground truth).
  • cuSOLVER FP32: same as cuSOLVER FP64, in single precision.
  • Polar Express FP32: composite filter , \(31\) GEMMs.
  • Polar Express FP16: composite filter , \(22\) GEMMs.
  • Newton–Schulz FP32: fixed-point filter , \(31\) GEMMs.
  • Newton–Schulz FP16: fixed-point filter , \(21\) GEMMs.
  • Composite FP32: our method in FP32, \(31\) GEMMs.
  • Composite FP32 (em.): same as Composite FP32, with BF16x9 emulation on B200.
  • Composite FP16: our method in FP16, \(22\) GEMMs.

Benchmark and Evaluation: Setup

Datasets. 33 dense datasets from the Matrix Depot package . For each dataset, generate three instances, of sizes \(5000\), \(10000\), and \(20000\).

Metrics. For the same matrix, both metrics are averaged over five runs.

  • Time: includes Lanczos iterations, data type conversion, and composite polynomial filtering.
  • Relative error: for input \(A\in\Sn^n\) and output \(A_+\), \[\frac{\norm{A_+-\PSDproj(A)}_F}{\norm{\PSDproj(A)}_F},\] where \(\PSDproj(A)\) is computed using cuSOLVER FP64.

Benchmark and Evaluation: Results

  • Our filters outperform other filters of the same precision in relative error.
  • In FP16, Composite FP16 reliably reaches \(10^{-3}\) relative error in milliseconds, over \(10\times\) faster than cuSOLVER FP64 and cuSOLVER FP32.
  • In FP32, cuSOLVER FP32 generally has the highest accuracy; Composite FP32 with emulation achieves speedups of up to \(2\times\).
